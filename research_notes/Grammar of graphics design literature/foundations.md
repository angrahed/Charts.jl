# Foundations: formal theory of the grammar of graphics and visualization specification languages

Scope: the formal theory behind grammar-of-graphics (GoG) systems and visualization specification languages: what each foundational work contributes, where they disagree, and what each implies for a new Julia grammar. The Julia grammar should be ggplot2-like but more customizable, give publication-quality output with very little code, and produce Highcharts-style interactive output. Notes compiled 2026-10-12. Each item gives its publication date. Where later work supersedes or qualifies an older one, the note says so. Local PDF paths are listed in the last section.

## Q1. What are the formal components of Wilkinson's *Grammar of Graphics*, and how did Wickham's layered grammar (2010) change it?

### Takeaway
Wilkinson's GoG (1st ed. 1999, 2nd ed. 2005) is a closed specification system. It has statements for DATA, TRANS, SCALE, COORD, ELEMENT and GUIDE, plus an algebra over variable sets ("varsets"): cross `*`, nest `/` and blend `+`. The algebra determines both the frame (which variables go on which dimension) and faceting. Within an ELEMENT, the geometry, statistic, position and aesthetics are nested into a single expression. Wickham's layered grammar (JCGS 2010) re-parameterised this around independent **layers** (data + mapping, stat, geom, position adjustment), plus one scale per aesthetic, a coordinate system and a facet specification. It **dropped** DATA, TRANS and the algebra, handing them to the host language (R and the reshape package). It **added** an explicit *hierarchy of defaults*. That hierarchy, not the grammar itself, is what makes ggplot2 concise.

### Cited findings
**Wilkinson's GoG (1999/2005)**
- Wikipedia lists Wilkinson's constructs as Variables ("mapping of objects to values represented in a graphic"), Algebra ("operations to combine variables and specify dimensions of graphs"), Geometry, Aesthetics ("sensory attributes"), Statistics, Scales ("represent variables on measured dimensions") and Coordinates. It quotes Wilkinson's hypothesis that these constructs are "orthogonal and virtually all known statistical charts can be generated relatively parsimoniously", and that the system "describes the meaning of what we do when we construct statistical graphics" rather than being a taxonomy. — [Wikipedia: Wilkinson's Grammar of Graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilkinson%27s_Grammar_of_Graphics)
- A secondary summary gives the GoG pipeline order as Variable → Algebra (varset algebra: cross, nest, blend) → Scales → Statistics → Geometry → Coordinates → Aesthetics → Renderer. It defines a varset by three requirements: a set of values, a set of objects, and a function mapping values to objects. — [pyG2 docs, "Graphic Generation Pipeline"](https://pyg2.readthedocs.io/en/latest/grammar_of_graphics/pipeline.html) (secondary; G2 itself did not adopt the algebra)
- **Formal varset definition and algebra (primary source: Wilkinson's patent, priority date 21 April 1999, granted 2007).** "varset[X₁,…,Xₙ] = {[l_m, X₁,…,Xₙ, f]}… f: l → X₁ × X₂ × …Xₙ". "Blend operator involves a union in the range." "The cross operator involves a Cartesian product in the range." "The nest operator stratifies values." "The rules for the operator include associativity, distributivity, and commutativity. Only blend is commutative." "The hierarchical order of the operators is blend (+), cross (*), and nest (/). Blend is evaluated last, while nest is evaluated first." The resulting tree is "ready" for a view: "Each 'level' of a tree represents a crossing dimension and the rows represent nesting dimensions." — [US7176924B2, "Graph algebra", L. Wilkinson](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7176924B2/en)
- In Wilkinson's GPL syntax, all parts of an element are intertwined. For example, `line(position(smooth.linear(x * y)), color(z))` corresponds to ggplot2's `layer(aes(x = x, y = y, color = z), geom = "line", stat = "smooth")`. Faceting is written inside the algebra plus COORD: `COORD: rect(dim(3), dim(1,2))` with `ELEMENT: point(position(x * y * z))`, versus `geom_point(aes(x, y)) + facet_grid(. ~ z)`. — [Wickham 2010, Figs. 5 and 9](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)
- The GoG distinguishes three spaces: frame, underlying space and display space (GoG 2nd ed., p. 359). — [Pollock & Satyanarayan 2025, GoFish, §4](https://vis.mit.edu/pubs/gofish.pdf)
- Wilkinson called the operations that avoid overlap (stack, dodge, jitter) *collision modifiers*. — [Wickham 2010 §3.1.4](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)
- Wilkinson's view (reported in 2017, published March 2022): "GoG was a complete, self-contained mathematical theory of statistical and scientific graphics"; "GoG is a mathematical system, and arguments against it need to be made on a mathematical basis"; "GoG is about formulating the meaning of a graphic in a way that a computer can understand enough to draw it and answer questions about it". Michael Friendly countered that implementations "are not all equivalent" in expressive power, elegance and extensibility, and that "there is much more to a statistical graphic than can be captured in the GoG… That part, which is poetry, cannot be captured in a mathematical theory of graphs". — [Friendly 2022, "Colorless Green Graphs Sleep Furiously", Nightingale](https://nightingaledvs.com/colorless-green-graphs-sleep-furiously-a-conversation-with-leland-wilkinson/)
- The GoG was implemented at SPSS/IBM as the nViZn platform (later GPL / VizML), built by a team Wilkinson assembled. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilkinson%27s_Grammar_of_Graphics); McNutt notes that Wilkinson himself said "any closed system will have missing pieces" and that his VizML "has limited support for nested or data-driven layouts (e.g. sets or cartograms) or mixed data and aesthetic-driven tasks (such as annotation)". — [McNutt 2022, arXiv:2207.07998](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.07998)

**Wickham's layered grammar (JCGS 19(1):3–28, 2010)**
- The paper presents an "alternative parameterization" of Wilkinson's grammar that differs "in its arrangement of the components, the development of a hierarchy of defaults, and in that it is embedded inside another programming language". — [Wickham 2010](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)
- Formal definition: a plot consists of "a default dataset and set of mappings from variables to aesthetics; one or more layers, with each layer having one geometric object, one statistical transformation, one position adjustment, and optionally, one dataset and set of aesthetic mappings; one scale for each aesthetic mapping used; a coordinate system; the facet specification". The components are "independent, meaning that we can generally change a single component in isolation". — [Wickham 2010 §3](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)
- A layer is the equivalent of Wilkinson's ELEMENT, but its parts are separate rather than intertwined. "This makes it possible to omit parts from the specification and rely on defaults: if the stat is omitted, the geom will supply a default; if the geom is omitted, the stat will supply a default; if the mapping is omitted, the plot default will be used." In Wilkinson's grammar "the dataset is implied by the variable names"; in the layered grammar it can be specified per layer. — [Wickham 2010 §3.1](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)
- **Stats**: "To make sense in a graphical context a stat must be location-scale invariant: f(x+a) = f(x)+a and f(b·x) = b·f(x)". A stat maps a dataset to a dataset, and new variables it computes (for example `..count..`) can be mapped to aesthetics. Stats "should be conditional on the coordinate system" (circular regression in polar coordinates, for example), but in practice they are computed in Cartesian space. "This issue is not discussed in Wilkinson's grammar." — [Wickham 2010 §3.1.2](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)
- **Geoms** are classified by dimensionality: 0d (point, text), 1d (path, line), 2d (polygon, interval). Every geom has a default stat and every stat a default geom. Location-based parameterisations are used internally because width and height make sense only in Cartesian coordinates. — [Wickham 2010 §3.1.3](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)
- **Scales**: "A scale is a function, and its inverse, along with a set of parameters"; "The inverse function is used to draw a guide". The layered scale is "equivalent to the SCALE and GUIDE of Wilkinson's grammar". *Annotation guides* "are not needed because they can be constructed with creative use of geoms… or… the underlying drawing system". Scaling happens in three steps: **transform** (before the stat, "so that statistics are computed on the scale-transformed data"), **train** (combine ranges across all facets and layers; local scaling makes cross-facet comparison meaningless) and **map**. This requires "two passes of scaling, before and after the statistical transformation". — [Wickham 2010 §§2.2, 3.2](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)
- **Coords** "affect all position variables simultaneously and differ from scales in that they also change the appearance of the geometric objects". They are applied after the stats. In the grammar a pie chart is a stacked bar in polar coordinates, and a Coxcomb is a bar chart in polar coordinates. — [Wickham 2010 §§3.3, 6.2](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)
- **Facets**: in Wilkinson's grammar, faceting "is an aspect of the coordinate system". In the layered grammar it is independent of layers and coordinates. That is "less flexible, as the layout of the facets always occurs in a Cartesian coordinate system, but in practice is not limiting". — [Wickham 2010 §3.4](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)
- **Hierarchy of defaults**: "There are defaults present in GPL, but they are not described in the Grammar of Graphics". The concrete defaults are: geom ↔ stat defaults; Cartesian coordinates by default; scales chosen "according to the aesthetic and type of variable". Continuous positions get a linear scale and categorical positions are mapped to integers. Continuous colour follows "a smooth path in the HCL color space"; discrete colour uses "evenly spaced hues with equal luminance and chroma". "The choice of a good default scale is difficult." Histogram bins default to 30, which Wickham calls "perverse", paired with a warning to make users choose a binwidth (parameterised by width, "directly interpretable on the graph"). — [Wickham 2010 §§4, 6.1](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)
- **What was dropped and why**: "By relying on other tools in R, ggplot2 does not need three elements of Wilkinson's grammar: DATA, TRANS, and the algebra." "The algebra describes how to reshape data for display and, in R, can be replaced by the reshape package… Separating data manipulation from visualization allows you to check the data, and the same restructuring can be used in multiple plots. Additionally, the algebra cannot easily perform aggregation or subsetting, but reshape can." Embedding costs something too: to preserve the grammar's "declarative nature" in R, ggplot2 uses `+` to add pieces to a base object, and that base object "is not necessary in a stand-alone grammar". — [Wickham 2010 §5](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)
- Transformations can happen in three places: data, scale and coordinate system. They produce visibly different results, which raises the question "should we be able to uniquely identify the specification of a graphic from its rendering?" — [Wickham 2010 §6.3](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)
- "A poetry of graphics": the grammar "offers no advice on how to write well". Wickham proposes a "grammar checker" that warns about too many variables (more than two positions plus one other aesthetic, so suggest faceting), overplotting, and alphabetical ordering of categories ("Alabama first!"). — [Wickham 2010 §7](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)
- **The descendants disagree about stacking**: "In the original GoG, stacking occurs through a collision modifier, yet ggplot2 accomplishes it via position adjustments, Vega-Lite with an encoding channel, and Observable Plot through a data transform. These inconsistencies and expressivity cliffs suggest there is important structure that the GoG does not capture." — [Pollock & Satyanarayan 2025](https://vis.mit.edu/pubs/gofish.pdf)
- **A Julia precedent for restoring an algebra**: AlgebraOfGraphics.jl combines `data(...)`, `mapping(...)` and `visual(...)`/analyses with `*` ("merge together" components within a layer) and `+` ("stack on top" as separate layers). It uses the distributive law, `(data * mapping) * (visual1 + visual2)`, to factor out what layers share. Scales are configured separately through `scales()`. — [AoG tutorial "Intro I"](https://aog.makie.org/dev/tutorials/intro-i); [AoG GitHub](https://github.com/MakieOrg/AlgebraOfGraphics.jl)

### Inferences
- The layered parameterisation with independently defaultable layer parts is the right core for "very little code". Wickham shows that the conciseness comes from the hierarchy of defaults (geom↔stat, type→scale, Cartesian coordinates, inherited plot-level data and mapping), which the GoG book never specified. A new grammar should *document the default hierarchy as part of the language semantics*, not leave it as an implementation detail.
- Wilkinson's algebra and AoG's algebra are different objects. Wilkinson's operates on **varsets and frames**: which variables share a dimension (blend), span dimensions (cross) or stratify (nest, which also governs faceting). AoG's operates on **specification fragments**: it merges or stacks partial layer specs. A Julia grammar can sensibly have both. One is a spec-composition algebra (AoG-style `*`/`+`, giving reuse and distributivity). The other is a small frame or facet algebra that keeps *nest* semantics ("only combinations present in the data", as in Polaris, Q3) and *blend* semantics ("several variables on one axis", for example several y series), which ggplot2 handles only through reshaping.
- Pin down where stacking, dodging and jittering live (position adjustment vs. transform vs. channel), because the descendants disagree. The GoFish critique suggests treating them as layout operators over groups rather than special cases (see Q6).
- Wickham's definition of a scale as "function + inverse" fits interactivity directly. Highcharts-style tooltips, crosshairs, zoom/brush and axis readouts all need pixel → data inversion. Make invertibility, or an explicit "non-invertible" flag, part of the scale contract.
- Keep the explicit ordering: scale transform → stat → train → map → coord transform. It is semantically load-bearing (log scale vs. log coord produce different smooths) and should be stated in the spec, not only in the code.
- The layered grammar dropped annotation guides. For publication-quality charts, annotations need to come back as first-class, data-anchored objects (see AnnoGram, Q6/Q7).

### Gaps
- I could not access Wilkinson's book (Springer, paywalled). The full list of GoG aesthetics is unverified here. From memory it covers position; form (size, shape, rotation); colour (hue, brightness, saturation); texture (granularity, pattern, orientation); optics (blur, transparency); and transformation/motion. Treat this as unverified. The algebra laws (identity elements, which side distributes, the exact unit/identity operations) are taken from a patent summary rather than the book chapter.
- I found no source that directly states Wilkinson's verdict on ggplot2's omission of the algebra. The 2022 interview reports his general position only.

## Q2. Bertin, Mackinlay (APT), Cleveland & McGill, and the replications: how should they inform default encodings?

### Takeaway
Bertin (1967) supplied the vocabulary: marks with planar and "retinal" variables, each with a level of organisation (associative, selective, ordered, quantitative). Mackinlay (1986) formalised it. A design is *expressive* if it encodes all the facts and only the facts. It is *effective* if it uses the most accurately perceived channel for the most important data, through a per-data-type ranking that extends Cleveland & McGill (1984), plus a "principle of importance ordering". Heer & Bostock (2010) replicated the C&M ranking by crowdsourcing and added layout defaults (gridline alpha, spacing, chart height). Work since 2018 shows the ranking depends on task, data distribution and individual, and that precision is not the only design goal. So defaults should follow the ranking, but as soft, overridable constraints rather than hard rules.

### Cited findings
- **Bertin's vocabulary, as summarised by Mackinlay (1986)**: marks are "points, lines, and areas", with Positional (1-D, 2-D, 3-D), Temporal (animation) and Retinal properties ("color, shape, size, saturation, texture, and orientation"). "The retinal properties are so called because the retina of the eye is sensitive to them, independent of the position of the object." — [Mackinlay 1986, Fig. 8](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/22949.22950)
- **Bertin's levels of organisation**: associative (group despite different values), selective (immediately isolate a group), ordered (rankable) and quantitative (estimate numeric difference). "Besides position… Bertin considered only size variation to be quantitative." Retinal variables have three implantations: point, line, area. — [Axis Maps, "Visual Variables"](https://www.axismaps.com/guide/visual-variables) (secondary)
- Polaris summarises Bertin's main contribution as "the identification of the retinal variables (position, color, size, etc.)" and Cleveland's as experimental ranking of "how well people can use these different retinal properties to compare quantitative variations". — [Stolte et al. 2002 §2.1](https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/polaris_extended/polaris.pdf)
- **Mackinlay's expressiveness criterion (1986)**: "A set of facts is expressible in a language if it contains a sentence that (1) encodes all the facts in the set, (2) encodes only the facts in the set." "Expressing additional information is potentially dangerous because it may not be correct." Graphical presentations are treated as "sentences of graphical languages" with "precise syntactic and semantic definitions". — [Mackinlay 1986 §§4–5](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/22949.22950)
- **Mackinlay's effectiveness criterion**: effectiveness "also depends on the capabilities of the perceiver". Without an empirically verified theory, "one must conjecture a theory of effectiveness". Its core is Cleveland & McGill's ranking for quantitative data. Mackinlay extended that ranking to ordinal and nominal data because "color, which is at the bottom of the quantitative ranking, is a very effective way of encoding nominal sets". He states that "this extension… has not been empirically verified". — [Mackinlay 1986 §6](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/22949.22950)
- Mackinlay's Fig. 15 ranking, as commonly reproduced; the PDF text extraction is partial, but the nominal column was verified. **Quantitative**: Position > Length > Angle > Slope > Area > Volume > Density > Colour saturation > Colour hue (texture, connection, containment and shape are marked not relevant). **Ordinal**: Position > Density > Colour saturation > Colour hue > Texture > Connection > Containment > Length > Angle > Slope > Area > Volume (shape is marked not relevant). **Nominal**: Position > Colour hue > Texture > Connection > Containment > Density > Colour saturation > Shape > Length > Angle > Slope > Area > Volume. — [Mackinlay 1986, Fig. 15](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/22949.22950)
- "Principle of Importance Ordering: Encode more important information more effectively." The input is an ordered tuple of relations, and the first relation "will get first chance at being matched to an effective primitive language". — [Mackinlay 1986 §§6, 8](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/22949.22950)
- **APT's composition algebra**: a "basis set of primitive graphical languages", each embodying one of Bertin's encoding techniques. The composition operators are *double-axes composition*, *single-axis composition* and *mark composition*. Synthesis uses partition → selection → composition with depth-first search and backtracking, implemented in logic programming. — [Mackinlay 1986 §§7–8](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/22949.22950)
- **Cleveland & McGill (JASA 1984)** ranking of elementary perceptual tasks, from most to least accurate: position along a common scale; position along non-aligned scales; length, direction, angle; area; volume, curvature; shading, colour saturation. — [Univ. of Iowa STAT4580 notes on C&M](https://homepage.divms.uiowa.edu/~luke/classes/STAT4580/percep.html) (secondary); original paper [DOI 10.1080/01621459.1984.10478080](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1984.10478080) (not open access)
- **Heer & Bostock crowdsourced replication (CHI 2010)**: "The ranking of types by accuracy is consistent" with C&M. "Position encoding still significantly outperformed length encoding." For the newly added angle (pie) and circular-area (bubble) judgments, "psychophysical theory predicts area to perform worse than angle, and both to be significantly worse than position… Theory also suggests that angle should perform worse than length, but the results do not support this." — [Heer & Bostock 2010a](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2010-MTurk-CHI.pdf)
- Heer & Bostock also found that rectangular area judgments (treemaps, cartograms) were as accurate as circular ones, and that aspect ratio 1:1 comparisons were *worst*. The authors explain this as "viewers use 1D length comparisons to help estimate area". — [Heer & Bostock 2010a, Exp. 1B](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2010-MTurk-CHI.pdf)
- Default-relevant layout findings from the same paper: the results "corroborate Stone & Bartram's recommendation of alpha = 0.2 as a 'safe' default" for gridlines. Charts 40 px tall caused significantly more error, with "little benefit for increasing chart height beyond 80 pixels". Gridlines improved accuracy, and "the results suggest that gridlines be separated by at least 8 pixels". — [Heer & Bostock 2010a, Exps. 2–3](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2010-MTurk-CHI.pdf)
- **Task and data dependence (Kim & Heer, EuroVis 2018; 1,920 crowd subjects; 12 trivariate encodings)**: "colored scatterplots (with positionally-coded quantities and color-coded categories) perform well for comparing individual points, but perform poorly for summary tasks as the number of categories increases". "Size is often considered preferable to color for encoding quantitative fields [CM84]. However… encoding a secondary quantitative information using size can impede decoding of a primary quantitative field that is spatially-encoded". The authors argue for "dynamic effectiveness criteria that are sensitive to task and data distributions". Traditional APT-style systems "use a greedy heuristic" that assigns the highest-priority field to the most effective channel. — [Kim & Heer 2018](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2018-TaskDataEffectiveness-EuroVis.pdf)
- **Individual differences (Davis et al., TVCG 2024; arXiv Dec 2022)**: canonical rankings come from "average observer" models. A Bayesian multilevel replication of C&M shows "some people show patterns of accuracy that credibly deviate from the canonical rankings". — [Davis et al., arXiv:2212.10533](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10533)
- **Knowledge drift (Zeng & Battle, CHI 2023)**: a review of 59 graphical-perception papers found "inconsistent reporting of findings and a lack of shared data across studies". "Cleveland and McGill treated pie charts as primarily angle encodings; however, more recent work suggests that pie charts are perceived more as area encodings", so systems built on a few papers "risk… making outdated decisions". The authors release a JSON knowledge base of rankings. — [Zeng & Battle 2023, arXiv:2109.01271](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01271)
- **Precision is not the only goal (Bertini, Correll & Franconeri, VIS 2020)**: the paper refutes "a caricature of this 'scatterplots only' argument", using Minard's map as the example. It argues that ordinal relations ("is A higher than B?") and aggregate or ensemble tasks matter as much as the metric ratio judgments C&M measured. — [Bertini et al. 2020, arXiv:2008.11310](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.11310)
- **Stronger critique (Reda et al., arXiv Aug 2026, preprint)**: "Visualization theory frames effectiveness at the level of the encoder… Human perception, however, operates as a fundamentally different decoder at the level of retinal images". Hence "experimental results and guidelines can be poor predictors of perceptual performance". The authors propose image-based vision models. — [Reda et al. 2026, arXiv:2608.07834](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07834) (not peer reviewed at time of writing)
- **Formalising design knowledge as constraints (Draco, Moritz et al., TVCG 2019)**: "modeling visualization design knowledge as a collection of constraints, in conjunction with a method to learn weights for soft constraints from experimental data", implemented in Answer Set Programming. The resulting models "can easily be augmented with additional constraints or updated weights". — [Moritz et al. 2019](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2019-Draco-InfoVis.pdf). Draco 2 (2023) is in the design folder.
- **Polaris's default retinal mappings (2002)**, a concrete precedent for default encodings. Shape uses a recommended set of shapes. Size uses "a linear map from the field domain to the area of the mark", with a minimum size chosen so all visual properties remain perceivable. Ordinal colour uses a predefined palette of well-separated colours. Quantitative colour uses an isomorphic colormap (Rogowitz). — [Stolte et al. 2002 §4.3](https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/polaris_extended/polaris.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Default channel assignment** for the Julia grammar can be Mackinlay-style: (a) measurement type (nominal/ordinal/quantitative, from Julia element types plus `CategoricalArrays` orderedness) restricts the expressive channels; (b) argument order supplies an importance ordering (x, y, then colour, then size or shape); (c) an effectiveness table picks channels. Expressiveness should be enforced (refuse, or warn on, quantitative → shape and nominal → size or area). Effectiveness should be soft and overridable.
- Because rankings drift (pie as area, task dependence, individual differences), implement defaults and lint rules as **data**: a small weighted constraint table, Draco-style, rather than hard-coded `if` chains, so they can be updated without changing the grammar. This also makes the "grammar checker" Wickham proposed in 2010 practical.
- Concrete, citable layout defaults: gridline alpha around 0.2 on light backgrounds; gridline spacing at least 8 px; for small multiples or sparklines, no benefit beyond about 80 px plot height for value comparison. The design/theming researcher should reconcile these with typography.
- Kim & Heer suggest cardinality-aware defaults. When a nominal colour variable has many levels and the likely task is summary or comparison of groups, prefer faceting or aggregation over a coloured scatter. A lint could warn above some cardinality threshold.
- Bertini et al. and Friendly's "poetry" point both warn against making "most precise" the only objective. Defaults should optimise for accuracy, but the grammar must not *forbid* less precise encodings (area, angle, Minard-style widths) that serve communicative goals.

### Gaps
- Bertin's book (1967; English 1983; Esri Press reprint 2010) is not freely available. The levels of organisation and the variable × level table are cited from secondary sources only.
- I did not verify the exact gray-box layout of Mackinlay's Fig. 15 from the scanned figure. The ordering above follows the common reproduction; the nominal column matches the extracted text.
- I found no large replication that ranks *colour hue vs. shape* for nominal data under modern display conditions in a form directly usable for defaults. Zeng & Battle's knowledge base may contain partial evidence; I did not examine it.

## Q3. Polaris/VizQL (2002) and Show Me (2007): what did they add to the GoG?

### Takeaway
Polaris (Stolte, Tang & Hanrahan, TVCG 2002) recast Wilkinson's algebra as a **table algebra** over database fields. Operands are ordinal fields (sets of domain values) and quantitative fields (singleton sets). The operators are concatenation `+`, cross `×` and nest `/`, and each expression normalises to a set that partitions an axis into rows, columns or layers. Every view compiles to relational (SQL/OLAP) queries. Its other additions were the dimension/measure distinction, mark families derived from axis field types (O-O, O-Q, Q-Q), layering of multiple data sources, and a shelf UI that writes algebra expressions implicitly. Show Me (Mackinlay, Hanrahan & Stolte, TVCG 2007) put APT-style automatic presentation into the commercial product (Tableau/VizQL). It extended it to tables of views (small multiples) and emphasised user experience in the analysis flow.

### Cited findings
- Polaris fields: "We can characterize fields… as nominal, ordinal, quantitative, or interval. Polaris reduces this categorization to ordinal and quantitative by treating intervals as quantitative and assigning an ordering to the nominal fields." Fields are also either dimensions or measures, "similar to independent and dependent variables". — [Stolte et al. 2002 §3](https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/polaris_extended/polaris.pdf)
- Table algebra: "A complete table configuration consists of three separate expressions… Two… define the configuration of the x and y axes of the table, partitioning the table into rows and columns. The third… defines the z axis of the table, which partitions the display into layers." Operand semantics: ordinal A ↦ domain {a₁…aₙ}, quantitative P ↦ {P}. "Ordinal fields will partition the table into rows and columns, whereas quantitative fields will be spatially encoded as axes within the panes." — [Stolte et al. 2002 §4.1](https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/polaris_extended/polaris.pdf)
- Operators, "listed in order of precedence": cross (Cartesian product), nest and concatenation ("ordered union"). Nest "only creates set entries for which there exist records with those domain values". For example, "quarter/month would be interpreted as those months within each quarter, resulting in three entries for each quarter. In contrast, quarter × month would result in 12 entries for each quarter." Each expression reduces to a "normalized set form" that "determines one axis of the table". — [Stolte et al. 2002 §4.1](https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/polaris_extended/polaris.pdf)
- **Differences from Wilkinson, in the authors' words**: Polaris was built "as much… as possible using relational algebra… All of the data transformations required by our visual specifications can be precisely interpreted as standard SQL queries". "Wilkinson instead intentionally uses a data model that is not relational… consisting of variable sets and indexed variables." "His blend operator performs both set union and may partition the axes of a table; our concatenation operation is different since it just performs partitioning… his cross and nest operators: cross generates a 2D graphic and nest only a 1D graphic." Polaris uses shelves to specify axes instead. — [Stolte et al. 2002 §8](https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/polaris_extended/polaris.pdf)
- **Precedence conflict**: Polaris gives the precedence as cross > nest > concatenation ([Stolte et al. 2002](https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/polaris_extended/polaris.pdf)). Wilkinson's patent says nest is evaluated first, then cross, then blend ([US7176924B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7176924B2/en)).
- Graphic taxonomy: "three families by the type of fields assigned to their axes: ordinal-ordinal, ordinal-quantitative, quantitative-quantitative". Mark choice within a family yields bar, line and similar charts. Marks are "rectangle, circle, glyph, text, Gantt bar, line, polygon, and image". Following Cleveland, graphics are further split by the number of independent and dependent variables. — [Stolte et al. 2002 §4.2](https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/polaris_extended/polaris.pdf)
- Multiple data sources map to distinct layers that share the table structure, which allows composite displays (for example a map layer plus a flights layer). — [Stolte et al. 2002 §4.1](https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/polaris_extended/polaris.pdf)
- Querying: one SQL query is needed per pane. The CUBE operator cannot be used because partitions overlap (as in scatterplot matrices). — [Stolte et al. 2002 §8](https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/polaris_extended/polaris.pdf)
- **Show Me (2007), abstract**: "an integrated set of user interface commands and defaults that incorporate automatic presentation into a commercial visual analysis system called Tableau. A key aspect of Tableau is VizQL, a language for specifying views, which is used by Show Me to extend automatic presentation to the generation of tables of views (commonly called small multiple displays). A key research issue for the commercial application of automatic presentation is the user experience, which must support the flow of visual analysis… The Show Me user experience includes the automatic selection of mark types, a command to add a single field to a view, and a pair of commands to build views for multiple fields… user interface logs indicate that Show Me is used by commercial users." — [Mackinlay, Hanrahan & Stolte 2007, PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17968057/)
- McNutt (2022) treats VizQL as evidence that GoG is "far from the only conceivable systematic model": "VizQL emphasizes data exploration through the language of a data cube." — [McNutt 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.07998)

### Inferences
- Polaris shows that a small, well-typed algebra over **categorical (partitioning) vs. continuous (axis-forming)** fields is enough to define faceting, nested axes and small multiples compactly. A Julia grammar could expose this as its facet/axis algebra, for example `rows = region / country`, `cols = year * quarter`, `y = sales + profit` (concatenation gives a multi-measure layout). Nest semantics (only observed combinations) is what ggplot2 approximates with `facet_wrap(drop = TRUE)` or `scales = "free"`.
- Polaris's "dimension vs. measure" split is a lightweight, user-understandable route to automatic mark selection ("Show Me"). For "excellent defaults with little code", a function such as `chart(df, :region, :sales)` with no geom could pick a mark from the (O,Q) family, as Show Me does, while staying fully overridable through the layered grammar.
- The SQL-compilation property matters for scalability and interactivity, because aggregation pushes down to the data source. The Julia design could keep stats as relational operations (group, aggregate, bin) so they can be executed lazily over DuckDB or Arrow; Mosaic (2024) does this on the web (see the interaction researcher's folder).
- Pick and document one precedence order. Polaris and Wilkinson disagree on cross vs. nest.

### Gaps
- The Show Me paper's full text (Tableau whitepaper behind a form; IEEE paywalled) was not accessible. Details such as the number of view types offered and the ranking heuristics used to choose among them could not be verified.
- I found no open publication formally specifying modern VizQL beyond Polaris 2002 and patents.

## Q4. Tidy data (Wickham 2014): how does data shape constrain a grammar's design?

### Takeaway
The GoG and its descendants assume a **single tidy table**: each variable a column, each observation a row, each type of observational unit a table. That assumption is what lets a specification be a mapping from columns to aesthetics. It also creates real limits. Wide or presentation-shaped data must first be reshaped (which Wilkinson's algebra once handled). Relational, multi-table data such as networks and hierarchies cannot be expressed faithfully. Data shape is the source of "schematic congruency" problems and silent errors in practice. Spreadsheet-style rich tables are not captured at all.

### Cited findings
- Definition: "each variable is a column, each observation is a row, and each type of observational unit is a table". "This is Codd's 3rd normal form… but with the constraints framed in statistical language." — [Wickham 2014, JSS 59(10)](https://www.jstatsoft.org/article/view/v059i10)
- The five common forms of messiness: "Column headers are values, not variable names. Multiple variables are stored in one column. Variables are stored in both rows and columns. Multiple types of observational units are stored in the same table. A single observational unit is stored in multiple tables." All can be fixed with "melting, string splitting, and casting". — [Wickham 2014 §3](https://www.jstatsoft.org/article/view/v059i10)
- On visualization: "Tidy visualization tools only need to be input-tidy as their output is visual. Domain specific languages work particularly well for the visualization of tidy datasets because they can describe a visualization as a mapping between variables and aesthetic properties of the graph… This is the idea behind the grammar of graphics." Some base-R functions (barplot, matplot, mosaicplot) and parallel coordinates work directly on "messy" wide data. — [Wickham 2014 §4.2](https://www.jstatsoft.org/article/view/v059i10)
- Wickham's 2010 justification for dropping the GoG algebra was that reshaping belongs in a separate data tool ([Wickham 2010 §5](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)). Tidy data is the other half of that design decision.
- **Schematic congruency (Satyanarayan et al. 2019)**: "authoring a visualization may require the backing dataset to be structured or formatted in a particular way that may not be clear to authors a priori… all three systems expect datasets to be structured in a long (often referred to as 'tidy'…) format". Non-tabular layouts (networks, geographies) "impose their own set of structuring and formatting concerns that are invisible to the author", for example links that must have fields named exactly `source` and `target`. Systems "must develop higher-level scaffolding that automatically infers or suggests appropriate transformations". — [Satyanarayan et al. 2019 §5.2](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2019-ReflectionsVisAuthoring-InfoVis.pdf)
- **Single-table assumption is unsound for relational data (Wu et al., arXiv Apr 2025)**: "Bertin's theory and its derivative grammars are predicated on a strong assumption: that the input is a single-table". Mapping multi-table data is "(L1) Tedious… (L2) Unsound… (L3) Limited Expressiveness". Their example: if edges are produced by join (N ⋈ E ⋈ N) and the points are then jittered or laid out by force, "the positions of the points and edges become inconsistent", a "violation of referential integrity". A single table "makes it impossible for existing formalisms to express seemingly common designs such as node-link diagrams, parallel coordinates, tree diagrams, nested visualizations, nor tree maps". They define a visualization as *faithful* "if it visually preserves the underlying database constraints" (schemas, keys, foreign keys). — [Wu et al. 2025, arXiv:2504.08979](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08979)
- **Untidy, rich tables (Bartram, Correll & Tory, TVCG 2022)**: tables carry "internal richness of meaning and special structural affordances that are often lost when they are imported or converted… annotations and other forms of metadata, but spatial organization, analytical transparency, and modes of interaction that do not survive distillation into the clean and tidy forms demanded by advanced analytics or visualization tools". The authors argue that "interactive tables are an important visualization idiom in their own right". — [Bartram et al. 2021, arXiv:2106.15005](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.15005)
- **Silent errors from data/spec coupling (Pu & Kay, CHI 2023, six TidyTuesday analysts)**: analysts "made hard-to-evaluate silent errors, where their data wrangling and visualization specifications implied different data semantics, producing plausible-looking plots without explicit errors". There is "a feedback loop and tight coupling between visualization and data transformation code". — [Pu & Kay 2023](https://xiaoyingpu.github.io/files/pu2023how.pdf)
- **Normalisation errors (Pu & Kay, CHI 2020)**: mapping `fill = cyl` onto a density of `mpg` makes ggplot2 piece together P(mpg | cyl) densities "without normalizing by cyl group counts P(cyl)", producing a misleading partition of the density. — [Pu & Kay 2020](https://doi.org/10.31219/osf.io/dy8qv)

### Inferences
- In Julia, accept any `Tables.jl` source. Treat a column as the unit of mapping, and make the long/tidy form canonical internally.
- To meet "very little code", provide built-in *blend*-style conveniences: `y = [:a, :b, :c]` (several columns as one variable, melted internally with a generated "series" key), much as Polaris concatenation and Wilkinson's blend did. Do not force users to reshape for multi-series charts, which is the most common reason ggplot2 users call `pivot_longer`.
- For networks, hierarchies and annotations, allow **multiple named datasets per chart with key references** between layers (edges reference node marks by key, not by copied coordinates). That keeps referential integrity when layouts, jitter or interactive dragging move nodes, which is Wu et al.'s faithfulness argument.
- Stats that compute proportions or conditionals (densities by group, stacked proportions) should define their normaliser explicitly. Otherwise, take a PGoG-style "probability expression" approach (see Q6) to avoid the silent errors Pu & Kay found.

### Gaps
- I found no study that measures how often reshaping is the main friction for GoG users at scale. Pu & Kay's study had six participants. McNutt et al. and Kruchten et al. touch on this but do not quantify it.

## Q5. Language design for visualization: declarative DSLs, Protovis, Munzner's models, the authoring-systems critique, and the expressiveness vs. ease-of-use trade-off

### Takeaway
The design literature gives a vocabulary for the central trade-off. Heer & Bostock (2009/2010) split it into **expressiveness** ("Can I build it?"), **efficiency** ("How long will it take?") and **accessibility/learnability** ("Do I know how?"), and argued for declarative DSLs at an *intermediate* level of abstraction. Declarative DSLs separate specification from execution, which enables optimisation and retargeting across platforms. Satyanarayan et al. (2019) used the same expressivity–learnability frame to critique three authoring systems. Wongsuphasawat (2020) mapped libraries onto a five-level abstraction ladder, with GoG grammars in the middle. Munzner's nested model (2009) and the Brehmer & Munzner typology (2013) put encoding/interaction design in context: it sits below task and data abstraction, and upstream errors cascade down.

### Cited findings
- **Declarative DSLs (Heer & Bostock, TVCG 2010)**: "By separating specification from execution, declarative languages can simplify development, enable unobtrusive optimization, and support retargeting across platforms". Designers "must choose between many visualization systems, balancing trade-offs among expressiveness ('Can I build it?'), efficiency ('How long will it take?') and accessibility ('Do I know how?')". GoG, ggplot2, HiVE and VizQL "provide a high level of abstraction and support rapid analysis, but do not provide fine-grained control over graphics and interaction. We target an intermediate level of abstraction that permits a wide array of custom visual designs, embeds within a host language, and yet avoids the tedium of low-level graphics tools." They also contribute "declarative specification of animated transitions" and rendering/event infrastructure "decoupled from the runtime platform". — [Heer & Bostock 2010b](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2010-Protovis-InfoVis.pdf)
- **Protovis model (2009, as described in 2010)**: "a mark is simply a collection of bound properties for an associated graphical form"; "a mark is generated once per associated datum". Panels contain and replicate marks. Marks support *prototypal inheritance*: "a new mark can be derived from an existing mark, inheriting its properties… override properties to specify new behavior", with "mix-in" extension. Protovis does not impose a toolkit-specific data model; it lets designers "mould the data" as they see fit. — [Heer & Bostock 2010b §3](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2010-Protovis-InfoVis.pdf); original Protovis paper in `literature/web-interactive/Bostock2009_protovis.pdf`
- **Critical reflections (Satyanarayan, Lee, Ren, Heer, Stasko, Thompson, Brehmer, Liu; TVCG 2020, presented at InfoVis 2019)** on Lyra, Data Illustrator and Charticulator: "expressivity and learnability" emerged "as pervasive dimensions". Threshold and ceiling are defined as "how difficult it is to learn how to use the system (threshold), and the expressive gamut of what can reasonably be achieved (ceiling)", and the authors use "learnability" in place of Bostock's "accessibility". Toolkits such as D3/Vega "achieve the ultimate expressivity" but need programming expertise. Grammars "allow authors to use concise and uniform languages" but "still pose a challenge as authors must use text". — [Satyanarayan et al. 2019](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2019-ReflectionsVisAuthoring-InfoVis.pdf)
- Specific lessons from the same paper. Charticulator deliberately does **not** auto-add legends: "while axes have a mostly uniform appearance… legends have a much more expressive design space" (direct labelling, for example), so auto-legends risk design fixation, at "a non-trivial learnability cost". Direct manipulation of axes and legends introduces "hidden dependencies", because "axes and legends provide the sole mechanism for reifying scale functions". Snapping and anchor constraints have "a learnability cost" but "specify more reusable designs". Data binding should filter to valid (expressive) bindings. Export as SVG/PNG is "lossy": "the link to the backing dataset is broken, and visualization semantics… is lost". Templates remain "hard-coded to the original dataset schema". The systems also assumed authors "come with a specific chart design in mind" (authoring, not designing), giving "largely linear interaction flows" and a "highly viscous" experience. — [Satyanarayan et al. 2019 §§4–5](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2019-ReflectionsVisAuthoring-InfoVis.pdf)
- **Library landscape (Wongsuphasawat, Nightingale, 22 Sept 2020)**: two axes, level of abstraction and API design. The five levels are (1) graphics libraries (p5.js, three.js); (2) low-level building blocks (D3, visx); (3) visualization grammars (Vega, Vega-Lite, G2); (4) high-level building blocks (ECharts, Highcharts, deck.gl); (5) chart templates (Chart.js, Google Charts). "Higher-level libraries usually require fewer lines of code… but then there are fewer things you can customize." API patterns: plain JS; JSON configuration (Vega, fully declarative and serialisable); JSON with callbacks (ECharts); framework-specific. — [Wongsuphasawat 2020](https://nightingaledvs.com/navigating-the-wide-world-of-data-visualization-libraries/) (essay/talk, not peer reviewed)
- **Nested model (Munzner, TVCG 2009)**: four levels, "characterize the task and data in the vocabulary of the problem domain, abstract into operations and data types, design visual encoding and interaction techniques, and create algorithms to execute techniques efficiently. The output from a level above is input to the level below… an upstream error inevitably cascades to all downstream levels". — [Munzner 2009](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/labs/imager/tr/2009/NestedModel/NestedModel.pdf)
- **Why/how/what typology (Brehmer & Munzner, TVCG 2013)**, the basis of Munzner's later what/why/how framework in *Visualization Analysis and Design* (2014). Tasks are described by **why** (consume [present, discover, enjoy] vs. produce; search [lookup, browse, locate, explore]; query [identify, compare, summarize]), **how** (encode; manipulate [select, navigate, arrange, change, filter, aggregate]; introduce [annotate, import, derive, record]) and **what** (inputs and outputs). The typology targets the "ends-means ambiguity" of terms such as filter and derive. — [Brehmer & Munzner 2013](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/labs/imager/tr/2013/MultiLevelTaskTypology/brehmer_infovis13.pdf)
- **JSON DSL survey (McNutt, TVCG 2023, VIS 2022)**: 57 JSON-style visualization DSLs. "The term 'visualization grammar' is not well defined and is used in a variety of ways." GoG-style models are "a safe default model for many visualization systems, as [they] 'expose(s) the mechanics of good practice'", but "far from the only conceivable systematic model". Extensions usually happen "in explicitly modeled features… precluding end-user modification of system concepts (e.g. new coordinate systems)". — [McNutt 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.07998); local copy in `literature/web-interactive/`
- **Measuring notations (Kruchten et al., 2023)**: proposes metrics computed over a gallery of examples across notations (40 visualisations × 9 notations, NotaScope tool) as an alternative to ad hoc gallery comparison or Cognitive Dimensions analysis. — [Kruchten et al. 2023, arXiv:2308.16353](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.16353); local copy in `literature/web-interactive/`
- **What analysts need beyond the GoG (Pu & Kay 2023)**: "participants who wanted to apply custom colors, positions, and angles faced difficulties because GoG only facilitates the mapping of data, not customizations, onto visual elements". Participants hard-coded data-space values to place annotations or resize elements, or mistakenly passed aesthetic values (hex colours) as data. They also built "informal plot templates". The authors recommend grammar support for templates and customisation. — [Pu & Kay 2023](https://xiaoyingpu.github.io/files/pu2023how.pdf)

### Inferences
- The Julia project's goals map onto the three Heer–Bostock axes. "Very little code / excellent defaults" is **efficiency + learnability** (low threshold). "More customizable than ggplot2" is the **ceiling**. "Interactive like Highcharts" is a **platform/retargeting** concern. A declarative spec separated from execution is the standard answer to the last: one spec, several renderers (SVG/PDF for publication, an interactive web backend).
- To avoid Wongsuphasawat's fixed trade-off (grammar level 3 vs. Highcharts level 4), aim for *layered access* within one system. Offer high-level chart functions that **expand to** grammar specs (like `qplot` → `layer`, or Show Me → VizQL), and grammar specs that expose a lower mark/scene level (Protovis-style marks with property inheritance) for customisation. The design rule is that every high-level shortcut must produce an inspectable, editable grammar object, so users never hit a cliff.
- Pu & Kay's data-space vs. aesthetic-space confusion argues for first-class *literal/identity aesthetics* (for example `color = literal("#a62d3b")`, `x = data_coord(…)`, `x = npc(0.9)` in normalised panel coordinates) and first-class *templates/themes* (spec fragments that can be partially applied, which an AoG-style algebra gives for free).
- Charticulator's legend reflection suggests treating legends as a scale guide with a *direct-labelling* alternative built in. Publication-quality defaults (direct labels on line charts) and Highcharts-style legend toggling both need a legend model richer than "one box per scale".
- Keep the spec semantic and serialisable so exports are not lossy: embed the spec and data references in the interactive output and allow re-targeting to new data with schema checks. That addresses the template brittleness noted by Satyanarayan et al.
- Munzner's model is a reminder that a grammar sits at the encoding/interaction level only. Defaults should be justified by the task abstractions they serve (lookup/compare/summarise), which ties back to Kim & Heer's task-dependent effectiveness.

### Gaps
- Wongsuphasawat's framework is a practitioner essay without empirical validation.
- I found no controlled study that quantifies the learnability cost of GoG-style text grammars vs. high-level chart-template APIs (Highcharts-style). Kruchten et al. offer metrics, not learnability outcomes.

## Q6. Extensions and limits of the GoG: what it cannot express well, and formal semantics

### Takeaway
The GoG handles statistical graphics of a single tidy table well. It is weak or "cliffy" for unit visualizations, layouts that wrap, nest, connect or overlap (waffles, mosaics, ribbons, scatterpies), networks and other relational data, annotations, rich tables, multi-view composition, and interaction. Each gap has spawned a domain grammar: Atom (unit visualisations), GoTree (trees), productplots (mosaics), SetCoLa (graphs), AnnoGram (annotations), ggtime (temporal semantics), PGoG and ggdibbler (probability and uncertainty), Vega-Lite selections and Reactive Vega (interaction), Animated Vega-Lite (animation). Formal-semantics work (Mackinlay's language view, Kindlmann & Scheidegger's algebraic design, Vickers et al.'s category theory, Bartonicek et al.'s monoids, Wu et al.'s faithfulness, Mason et al.'s continuity) gives *criteria* a grammar should satisfy rather than a single agreed denotational semantics.

### Cited findings
**Layout limits: unit visualisations and Gestalt structure**
- Atom (Park, Drucker, Fernandez & Elmqvist, TVCG 2018): "Unit visualizations are a family of visualizations where every data item is represented by a unique visual mark… Current visualization grammars cannot fully describe the unit visualization family". Atom is "based on passing data through a series of layout operations that divide the output of previous operations recursively until the size and position of every data point can be determined". — [Park et al. 2018, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29990044/); author manuscript [PMC6995670](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6995670/)
- Atom on the GoG: it "incorporates many of the concepts of Wilkinson's Grammar of Graphics… such as mark selection, statistical calculation, and aesthetics. However, in the Grammar of Graphics, only collision modifiers such as dodging and stacking are used to avoid the overlapping, which limits the range of potential layouts… in contrast to GoG-style grammars, which tend to specify layouts implicitly based on mark choice, visualizations in Atom emerge from a combination of low-level grouping and primitive layout operations." It also states that "GoG and ggplot2 are focused on visual specification and do not provide the interaction operations necessary for truly interactive graphics". — [Park et al. 2018 (PMC full text)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6995670/)
- GoFish (Pollock & Satyanarayan, TVCG, published Dec 2025): "even seemingly simple charts like mosaics, waffles, and ribbons fall out of scope of most GoG implementations". To author them, designers "must either rely on overburdened grammar developers to implement purpose-built mark types (thus reintroducing the issues of typologies) or drop to lower-level frameworks". The scatterpie needs `geom_scatterpie`, written in ggproto. GoFish "formalizes Gestalt principles (e.g., uniform spacing, containment, and connection) that have heretofore been complected in GoG constructs" as graphical operators (`Stack`, `Connect`, `Enclose`, plus `Frame` for layering) that "can be nested and overlapped arbitrarily", "while retaining the benefits of high-level grammars like scale resolution and coordinate transform management". The pipeline runs Data → Underlying → Display space (adapting GoG's frame/underlying/display). Domain grammars (GoTree, Atom, productplots, SetCoLa) "are more tailored… and more concise than GoFish", but "switching between grammars presents large complexity cliffs". — [Pollock & Satyanarayan 2025](https://vis.mit.edu/pubs/gofish.pdf)
- Bluefish (Pollock et al., UIST 2024) is the diagramming precursor: "relations: declarative, composable, and extensible diagram fragments that relax the concept of a UI component. Unlike a component, a relation does not have sole ownership over its children nor does it need to fully specify their layout", rendered via "a compound graph that captures both hierarchical and adjacent relationships". — [Pollock et al., arXiv:2307.00146](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.00146)

**Relational data, networks, multi-table**
- Wu et al. (2025): single-table grammars cannot faithfully express node-link diagrams, trees, treemaps or nested visualisations; they define faithfulness as preservation of database constraints. — [arXiv:2504.08979](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08979) (see Q4)

**Annotations**
- Wickham (2010) left annotation guides out of the grammar, to be built "with creative use of geoms" or the drawing system ([Wickham 2010 §3.2](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)). AnnoGram (Rahman, Rahat-uz-Zaman, McNutt & Rosen, arXiv July 2025) responds that "most visualization tools treat [annotations] as secondary constructs — manually defined, difficult to reuse, and loosely coupled to the underlying visualization grammar". It proposes "a declarative extension to Wilkinson's Grammar of Graphics that reifies annotations as first-class design elements, enabling structured specification of annotation targets, types, and positioning strategies", prototyped as Vega-Lite Annotation. — [AnnoGram, arXiv:2507.04236](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.04236); local copy in `literature/implementations/`

**Multi-view composition**
- Composite visualisations combine views through juxtaposition, overlay and nesting. Deng et al. (2022) re-survey their design patterns. — [Deng et al., arXiv:2203.10476](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.10476). Wickham's facets cover only same-spec small multiples in a Cartesian grid ([Wickham 2010 §3.4](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)). Vega-Lite's view algebra (concat/repeat/layer/facet) is the main GoG extension here; the web-grammar researcher covers it (`literature/web-interactive/Satyanarayan2017_vega-lite.pdf`).

**Tables**
- Bartram et al. argue that interactive tables are "an important visualization idiom in their own right" that tidy pipelines flatten. — [arXiv:2106.15005](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.15005). Structured reporting tables have their own faceted-computation frameworks, for example rtables (Becker & Waddell 2023). — [arXiv:2306.16610](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.16610)

**Interaction and animation (brief)**
- Bartonicek, Urbanek & Murrell (JCGS 34(3), 2025; published online 2024): "while we would often like to treat graphics, statistics, and interaction in our plots as independent, they are in fact deeply connected… if we want our graphics to support interactive features which split our data into parts and then combine these parts back together (such as linked selection), then the statistics underlying our plots need to possess certain properties", namely group or monoid structure. — [Bartonicek et al. 2025, DOI 10.1080/10618600.2024.2429708](https://doi.org/10.1080/10618600.2024.2429708) (abstract only; paywalled)
- Heer & Bostock (2010) extended Protovis with declarative animated transitions. — [Heer & Bostock 2010b](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2010-Protovis-InfoVis.pdf). Other researchers cover grammars of interaction (Vega-Lite selections, Reactive Vega) and animation (Gemini, Animated Vega-Lite) in `literature/web-interactive/` and `literature/interaction/`.

**Probability, uncertainty, time**
- PGoG (Pu & Kay, CHI 2020; Best Paper Honorable Mention): "an extension to Wilkinson's original framework" that "makes probability expressions, such as P(A|B), a first-class citizen in the language… expressive, encompassing product plots, density plots, icon arrays, and dotplots… Its coherent syntax ensures correctness (that the proportions of visual elements and their spatial placement reflect the underlying probability distribution) and reduces edit distance". — [Pu & Kay 2020](https://doi.org/10.31219/osf.io/dy8qv)
- ggdibbler (Mason, Cook, Goodwin & VanderPlas, arXiv June 2026): a "mathematical framework for visualisation, which argues that we should consider visualisations to be continuous functions"; for random-variable inputs, "the visualisations should obey the continuous mapping theorem". This yields "a complete integration of uncertainty into the grammar of graphics", implemented as a ggplot2 extension where the data of any plotting function can be replaced by a random variable. — [Mason et al. 2026, arXiv:2606.24217](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24217); local copy in `literature/implementations/`
- ggtime (Huang, O'Hara-Wild, Hyndman & Kay, arXiv Oct 2025): a "grammar of temporal graphics" with "composable elements that support visualization across linear, cyclical, quasi-cyclical, and other granularities; standardization of irregular durations; and alignment of time points across different granularities and time zones". — [arXiv:2510.25656](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.25656); local copy in `literature/implementations/`

**Formal semantics and algebraic treatments**
- Mackinlay (1986): graphical presentations as "sentences of graphical languages" with precise syntax and semantics. Expressiveness is a formal property of the language; effectiveness is a (conjectured) perceptual property. — [Mackinlay 1986](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/22949.22950)
- Kindlmann & Scheidegger (TVCG 2014), algebraic visualization design: three spaces (data D, representation R, visualization V) and a commutative diagram **v ∘ r₂ ∘ α = ω ∘ v ∘ r₁**, where α are data symmetries and ω visualization symmetries. It yields three principles. **Representation Invariance**: "changing the representation should not change the visualization"; a failure is a *hallucinator*. **Unambiguous Data Depiction**: "changing the underlying data should produce a change"; a failure is a *confuser*. **Visual-Data Correspondence**: "significant changes in the data should meaningfully correspond with noticeable changes in the visual impression and vice versa"; failures are *jumblers* and *misleaders*. The authors relate these to Mackinlay: inexpressiveness arises from confusers, hallucinators or misleaders, and ineffectiveness from jumblers. — [Kindlmann & Scheidegger 2014](https://vis.cs.ucdavis.edu/vis2014papers/TVCG/papers/2181_20tvcg12-kindlmann-2346325.pdf)
- Vickers, Faith & Rossiter (TVCG 2013) combine semiotics (Saussure, Peirce) with category theory to formally define "literalness, sensitivity, redundancy, ambiguity, generalizability, and chart junk", generalising Ziemkiewicz & Kosara. — [Vickers et al. 2013, arXiv:1311.4376](https://arxiv.org/abs/1311.4376)
- Wickham's location-scale-invariance requirement for stats ([Wickham 2010](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)) and Bartonicek et al.'s monoid requirement for interactively re-aggregated stats ([JCGS 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/10618600.2024.2429708)) are both algebraic *laws on the stat component*.
- Wilkinson's algebra is itself a formal system (varsets with associativity and distributivity, blend commutative). — [US7176924B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7176924B2/en). AlgebraOfGraphics.jl adds an algebra over specs in which `*` distributes over `+`. — [AoG](https://aog.makie.org/dev/tutorials/intro-i)

### Inferences
- The Julia grammar should **separate "what a mark looks like" from "where it is arranged"** (GoFish/Atom) at least internally. Stacking, dodging, waffling, packing, treemap subdivision and connecting (ribbons, slopegraphs, Sankey links) can then be composable layout operators over groups rather than a growing zoo of geoms. Expose them through ggplot-like position arguments for the common case (`position = stack()`), but make them nestable (stack inside a facet, scatter pies made of stacked wedges).
- Treat **annotations as first-class layers with targets** (data selectors or keys), types and placement strategies (AnnoGram), positioned in data, panel-normalised or pixel coordinates. That serves both publication quality (labelled points, reference lines, callouts) and interactivity (tooltips are transient annotations attached to a hovered target).
- Build **interaction into the semantics**, not as an afterthought. Bartonicek et al. imply that if Highcharts-style legend toggling or linked brushing re-aggregates (a stacked bar recomputed after hiding a series), each stat must declare whether it is monoidal (sums and counts are, so they can be updated incrementally) or must be recomputed (medians, quantiles). Kindlmann's invariance principle suggests a test harness: permuting rows, relabelling levels or changing numeric representation must not change the picture.
- A *grammar of graphics + layout operators + annotations + selections* core with domain extensions (time, uncertainty, networks) via key-referenced datasets covers most of the limits above without forking into separate grammars.

### Gaps
- I found no complete denotational semantics (spec → image function with proven compositional properties) for ggplot2, Vega-Lite or the GoG. Existing work gives algebraic *principles* (Kindlmann), *categorical framings* (Vickers) or *operator algebras* (Wilkinson, AoG), not a full semantics.
- Atom's full-text PDF could not be downloaded: Microsoft Research and PMC block scripted downloads. Its operator list (grid, pack, treemap, gravity and so on) was not verified in detail.
- I did not review the grammars of tables (gt's "grammar of tables" claims, for example) in primary sources.

## Q7. Significant 2020–2026 theoretical work refining or critiquing the GoG, and where foundational works disagree

### Takeaway
Since 2020, theory has moved in three directions. **Semantic extensions** add new first-class concepts to the GoG: probability (PGoG 2020), uncertainty as random-variable input (ggdibbler 2026), time (ggtime 2025), annotation (AnnoGram 2025), databases and relations (Wu 2025). **Structural critiques** say the GoG's layout model is complected and cliffy (GoFish 2025, building on Atom 2018 and Bluefish 2024) and that "grammar" is ill-defined, with the GoG one model among many (McNutt 2022). **Effectiveness critiques** say channel rankings are task-, data- and person-dependent and possibly the wrong level of analysis (Kim & Heer 2018; Bertini et al. 2020; Davis et al. 2024; Zeng & Battle 2023; Reda et al. 2026). Empirical work on real use (Pu & Kay 2023) and a 2026 "grammar of graphical actions" question whether a finished declarative spec is the right unit at all.

### Cited findings
- **2020, PGoG**: probability expressions as first-class grammar objects; correctness by construction for proportions. — [Pu & Kay 2020](https://doi.org/10.31219/osf.io/dy8qv)
- **2020, beyond precision**: "scatterplots only" is a caricature; ordinal and ensemble tasks matter. — [Bertini et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.11310)
- **2021/2022, untidy data**: rich tables as an idiom; tidy-pipeline assumptions lose meaning. — [Bartram et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.15005)
- **2022/2023, no grammar to rule them all**: "visualization grammar" is ill-defined; GoG is "a safe default model" but "far from the only conceivable systematic model"; 57 JSON DSLs surveyed. — [McNutt 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.07998)
- **2022/2024, risks of ranking**: individuals credibly deviate from the canonical rankings. — [Davis et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10533)
- **2023, perception knowledge base**: inconsistent reporting; pie-as-area vs. angle; JSON collation for recommender systems. — [Zeng & Battle 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01271)
- **2023, GoG in practice**: GoG matches iteration but not customisation; silent errors from spec/wrangling coupling. — [Pu & Kay 2023](https://xiaoyingpu.github.io/files/pu2023how.pdf)
- **2023, notation metrics**: gallery-based metrics for comparing notations. — [Kruchten et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.16353)
- **2024, Bluefish**: relations that do not own their children, as a diagram primitive. — [Pollock et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.00146)
- **2024/2025, groups and monoids**: graphics, statistics and interaction are interdependent; linked selection requires algebraic properties of stats. — [Bartonicek et al.](https://doi.org/10.1080/10618600.2024.2429708)
- **2025, database visualization formalism**: faithfulness = preservation of schema and key constraints; single-table grammars are "unsound" for relational data. — [Wu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08979)
- **2025, AnnoGram**: annotations as first-class GoG elements. — [Rahman et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.04236)
- **2025, ggtime**: temporal semantics (granularities, cycles, time zones) as grammar elements. — [Huang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.25656)
- **2025, GoFish**: Gestalt-based graphical operators with recursive composition; GoG implementations disagree on what stacking is. The authors claim to point "towards an updated theory of visualization, one that is open to an innumerable space of graphic representations instead of limited to a fixed set of 'good' designs". — [Pollock & Satyanarayan 2025](https://vis.mit.edu/pubs/gofish.pdf)
- **2025, SGL**: a SQL-styled grammar of graphics compared against the layered grammar. — [Chapman, arXiv:2505.14690](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14690)
- **2026, ggdibbler**: visualisations as continuous functions obeying the continuous mapping theorem for random-variable inputs. — [Mason et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24217)
- **2026, image-level critique**: stop running encoder-level perception studies and model visualisations as images. — [Reda et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07834) (preprint)
- **2026, ggaction**: "A chart may be declarative; authoring it is not." Authoring is modelled as a chain of action functions. The authors claim better human and machine (LLM) interpretability than Vega-Lite and ggplot2. — [Jeon & Seo, arXiv:2609.14353](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.14353) (preprint, Sept 2026)
- **2026, Vega-Video**: integrating video synchronisation, annotation and transformation into the Vega grammar. — [Winecki & Nandi, arXiv:2604.24958](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.24958)

**Main disagreements across the foundational works**
1. *Is the GoG a complete theory?* Wilkinson says yes: "complete, self-contained mathematical theory" ([Friendly 2022](https://nightingaledvs.com/colorless-green-graphs-sleep-furiously-a-conversation-with-leland-wilkinson/)). Friendly says the "poetry" lies outside it (same source). McNutt calls it "far from the only conceivable systematic model" ([2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.07998)). GoFish says it is "our most comprehensive formal theory" but has "cliffs" ([2025](https://vis.mit.edu/pubs/gofish.pdf)).
2. *The algebra.* Wilkinson keeps it (varsets; cross/nest/blend). Wickham drops it in favour of host-language reshaping, because the algebra "cannot easily perform aggregation or subsetting" ([2010](https://vita.had.co.nz/papers/layered-grammar.pdf)). Polaris turns it into a relational table algebra ([2002](https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/polaris_extended/polaris.pdf)). AoG reintroduces an algebra over specifications ([docs](https://aog.makie.org/dev/tutorials/intro-i)). Operator precedence differs: Polaris has cross > nest > concatenation; Wilkinson evaluates nest first, then cross, then blend.
3. *Where layout lives.* Collision modifier (GoG) vs. position adjustment (ggplot2) vs. encoding channel (Vega-Lite) vs. data transform (Observable Plot) vs. Gestalt operators (GoFish) vs. recursive layout operations (Atom).
4. *Faceting.* Part of the algebra and COORD (Wilkinson), an independent component (Wickham), or table-axis expressions (Polaris).
5. *Effectiveness.* A single ranking (C&M 1984; Mackinlay 1986, explicitly "conjectural") vs. task- and data-dependent (Kim & Heer 2018) vs. person-dependent (Davis et al.) vs. not only precision (Bertini et al.) vs. the wrong level of analysis (Reda et al.).
6. *Data model.* Non-relational varsets (Wilkinson) vs. relational/OLAP (Polaris) vs. a single tidy table (Wickham 2014) vs. multi-table with constraints (Wu 2025) vs. rich human tables (Bartram 2021).
7. *Abstraction level.* High-level grammars (GoG, ggplot2, VizQL) vs. intermediate-level mark languages (Protovis, Heer & Bostock 2010) vs. claims to span both through recursive composition (GoFish).

### Inferences
- The newest work points to a design pattern that suits the Julia project. Use a **small orthogonal core** (data refs, mappings, scales with inverses, stats with declared algebraic properties, marks, layout operators, coordinates, guides and annotations, selections) and put **domain semantics in typed variables** (time types, distributions, categorical with order, keyed relations). Julia's type system and multiple dispatch suit this well: ggtime- and ggdibbler-style behaviour could come from dispatch on column eltypes (`Date`/`ZonedDateTime`, `Distribution`) rather than new geoms.
- ggaction suggests making the authoring *history* (a chain of edits) a first-class, serialisable object alongside the final spec. That helps LLM-assisted authoring and undo/redo in interactive editors. This is an emerging idea (preprint).
- Defaults should come from a revisable knowledge base, not from claimed universal rankings.

### Gaps
- Several 2025–2026 items (Reda et al., ggaction, Mason et al., AnnoGram, ggtime, SGL, Vega-Video) are arXiv preprints whose peer-review status was not verified. GoFish was published in TVCG (DOI 10.1109/TVCG.2025.3634250).
- I found no 2020–2026 work that proposes a full formal semantics unifying the GoG with interaction. Bartonicek et al. is the closest, and only its abstract was accessible.
